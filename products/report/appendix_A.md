# Appendix A. Home advantage over two decades (A1, descriptive)

**One-sentence conclusion.** Pooled home advantage falls from a 37.9% multiplicative boost in 2003/04 to 19.3% in 2024/25, a weighted slope of −0.090 per decade (95% CI −0.096 to −0.084), the slope is negative in all six leagues, and γ drops by 0.139 log points in the empty-stadium window, about 7.8 times the ordinary year-to-year standard deviation, before partly recovering.

### A.1 What this appendix answers

A1 is a descriptive robustness analysis, not a hypothesis. It asks whether the home-advantage parameter of the score model is stable over time. B3 and B4 assume a single γ for the whole training window, so if γ has drifted, the model is mis-specified in a way that matters for every fold. The appendix estimates the drift, compares the empty-stadium period with the seasons on either side, and checks what a stale γ costs when extrapolated to 2024/25.

**No causal claim is made.** The 2020/21 season is a single observation and coincides with a mid-season suspension, a compressed calendar and a change in substitution rules, so the empty-stadium window is a descriptive contrast, not a natural experiment.

### A.2 Data and method

**Series (A1-a).** One γ per season, estimated separately in each league from every match up to the end of that season (an expanding window). All 22 points use the same time-decay setting, **ξ = 0.0019** (one-year half-life), and no low-score correction: letting ξ or ρ vary by season would mix tuning noise into the trend. Standard errors come from the observed information matrix of the negative log likelihood, evaluated on the reduced parameterisation and therefore already marginalised over the attack and defence parameters. The trend is a weighted least-squares fit of γ on season index with weights 1/se².

**Windows (A1-b).** Windows are cut on **dates, not seasons**, because 2019/20 was interrupted in March 2020 and resumed in June: pre = 2018-08-01 to 2020-06-01, empty = 2020-06-01 to 2021-08-01, post = 2021-08-01 to 2023-07-01. Within each window γ is fitted with ξ = 0, per league, then pooled across leagues by inverse variance. Leagues share no matches, so pooling six separate fits avoids assuming a common scoring level. Windows share no matches either, so the standard error of a difference is the quadrature sum of the two window standard errors.

**Extrapolation (A1-c).** All parameters are fitted on history up to 2023/24 and then frozen. The 2024/25 season is predicted twice: with the stale γ inherited from history, and with the γ that the 2024/25 data themselves would select (an oracle used only as a diagnostic). The difference in log loss is the cost of treating home advantage as a constant.

**Code.** `src/models/a1_gamma.py`, `src/models/a1_empty.py`, `src/models/a1_extrapolation.py`; the analysis and both figures are in `analysis/a1_home_advantage.ipynb`. Tables are regenerated by `python -m src.models.m3_report_tables`.

Early points are noisy because they use few matches (2,058 in 2003/04 against 46,507 by 2024/25), which is why their standard errors fall from 0.027 to 0.0055 and why the weighted fit is dominated by the recent, better-measured seasons.

Across the six leagues the oracle γ improves log loss in five, by 0.0028 nats per match when pooled over 2,058 matches. The stale γ overstates home advantage on average (0.213 against 0.196) and predicts a 44.3% home-win share against an actual 42.5%; the oracle predicts 43.7%. The per-league direction is mixed: Δγ is positive in three leagues (E0, I1, D1) and negative in three (SP1, F1, N1), so this is a directional check consistent with the decline in A1-a, not a hypothesis test.

### A.4 What this appendix can and cannot say

1. **The trend intervals are too narrow, not too wide.** Consecutive points share most of their matches, so their errors are positively correlated; the weighted least-squares interval treats them as independent. A simulation with AR(1) errors calibrated to the 22 standard errors (`products/tables/tA1_trend_se_inflation.csv`, 4,000 draws, seed 0) finds the true slope standard deviation is 1.7 times the naive one at ρ = 0.5 and 3.3 times at ρ = 0.9. Even the larger factor leaves the pooled slope 8.7 standard errors from zero, so the sign and the league-by-league agreement survive, but the quoted 95% interval should be read as optimistic.
2. **The empty-stadium window is descriptive.** It contains one season and is confounded by the suspension, the compressed calendar and the substitution-rule change. The 7.8-times-normal drop is large and one-sided across leagues, but it cannot be attributed to crowd absence alone.
3. **The decline is a trend, not a monotone law.** Pooled γ falls from 0.182 in 2020/21 to 0.176 in 2024/25, but it rises in between, to 0.225 in 2022/23, and two leagues (F1, N1) rise in the final season. A single linear slope summarises the direction, not the path.
4. **ξ is fixed.** All 22 points use ξ = 0.0019 and no low-score correction. Changing ξ requires re-running the whole series and the trend together; it is not a per-season choice, and no value was selected by looking at the slope.

The appendix supports one claim only: the home-advantage term in B3/B4 is not constant over the sample, and its decline is large relative to ordinary season-to-season variation. It does not identify why, and it does not enter any hypothesis test.
